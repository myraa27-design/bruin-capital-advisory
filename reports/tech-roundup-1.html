November 2026: Tech Roundup

By: Aditya Arora, Jacob Ralph, Nathan Ma, Som Chandrashekhar

A New Winner in the LMS Space

The healthcare industry is facing a persistent and growing labor shortage. With a deficit of about 5 million nurses, hospitals are under pressure to onboard, train, and upskill their clinical staff faster than ever. Because there is a physical limitation on the availability of healthcare professionals, hospitals must make their existing workforce more productive, identify skill gaps earlier, and reduce the time and money spent on training. This creates an interesting opportunity in the healthcare Learning Management System (LMS) space, specifically for HealthStream (NASDAQ: HSTM).

To begin with an overview, HealthStream provides subscription-based software and educational content that hospitals use to train, certify, and develop their clinical staff. Its products help healthcare organizations manage training, clinical competencies, credentialing, and regulatory compliance.

HealthStream’s moat is its data:

  1. NurseGrid: Acquired in 2020, NurseGrid gives HealthStream access to a large network of nurses and insights into their preferences, such as when nurses prefer to work, their availability of their shifts, and what types of people they work best with. Today, roughly 1 in 5 nurses use NurseGrid, with 2,000 new nurses being added to their app per week.
  2. HealthStream's technical database: Collected over decades, HealthStream collects information on the technical competencies of healthcare professionals. This includes clinical assessments, learning modules, trainings, and certification checks. This allows hospitals to identify where nurses are proficient and where additional training is required. Once a lack of proficiency is recognized, Jane, HealthStream’s AI chatbot, recommends training modules for nurses to complete.

Let’s walk through a scenario to see how different parts of HealthStream’s business connect: consider a nurse who is proficient in administering IVs but needs additional CPR training. HealthStream's clinical data will identify this gap. NurseGrid will provide context on the nurse's availability. Jane will understand this information and assign modules to remedy it.

Right now, Jane is just a chatbot. Therefore, it’s interesting to consider what an agentic and fully automated platform could do to coordinate the appropriate training and communicate progress to the relevant manager. If HealthStream can eventually automate more of the process from assessment to training and validation, hospitals could reduce unnecessary training hours while ensuring employees develop the skills they actually need. This is particularly valuable in a market where labor is both expensive and increasingly difficult to find.

Earlier in the year, investors were concerned that AI could undermine traditional SaaS business models in two ways. First, companies could require fewer employees as AI automates more work. For software providers that charge on a per-user basis, this could translate into fewer paying subscribers. Second, AI could make software and educational content cheaper to develop, lowering barriers to entry and increasing competition.

HealthStream was caught up in this broader concern, with shares falling to a 52-week low of approximately $19.50. We know this to be true, as this drop in share price corresponds directly with IGV, the software ETF.

However, HealthStream's business is different from the average enterprise software company. Nurses and other clinical professionals cannot simply be replaced by AI, and healthcare organizations still need to maintain training, certification, and regulatory compliance. In addition, HealthStream's accumulated clinical content, competency data, and established hospital relationships make it impossible for a new competitor to replicate. The data is what makes HealthStream unique, not the complexity of its model.

The market realized this after Q2, when the company reported stronger results. In Q2 2026, HealthStream grew revenue 12.5% year over year to $83.7 million, while operating income increased 41.4%. Additionally, investor confidence received another boost in September when WJRJJ Ventures, owned by Copart founder Willis Johnson, purchased approximately $40 million of newly issued HealthStream shares at $29.50 per share. This led to the stock recovering to approximately $29.74 in October.

Interestingly, let’s examine HealthStream’s P/E ratio during the above events:

MetricMay 2026October 2026
Stock price~$20.00~$29.74
Earnings per share (approx. trailing)$0.70$0.72
P/E ratio~29x~41x

While earnings increased only modestly, the stock price increased nearly 50%, resulting in a significant expansion in its P/E multiple. (Remember: A P/E ratio represents how much investors are willing to pay for a share of the company)

Therefore, in other words: investors became willing to pay substantially more for nearly the same earnings. This suggests that at least part of the recovery came from changing expectations about AI's impact on the business, rather than an equivalent increase in profitability.

At first glance, a P/E of approximately 41x appears expensive for a company growing revenue at a high-single-digit rate. However, HealthStream's accounting provides additional context.

The company capitalizes a portion of its software development costs and amortizes them over time. This amortization reduces reported earnings without representing an equivalent cash expense in the current period. To fix this problem, we can examine HealthStream's EV/EBITDA multiple. After calculating it, we land on an 11-12x EV/EBITDA, a much more moderate multiple for a subscription-driven software company with recurring revenue.

To be clear, this does not automatically make the stock undervalued, particularly because ongoing software development still requires cash investment. However, it does suggest that the P/E ratio that many people have a problem with does not tell the entire story.

The latest Q3 results (as of last week) also show that the growth trajectory is not entirely smooth. Revenue grew 8.9% year over year, but operating income declined 13%. This makes sustained profitability improvement an important factor to monitor going forward.

How AI Could Change Online Shopping

Artificial intelligence is beginning to change how people shop online, moving beyond suggesting products to actually buying them for customers. This new technology, known as agentic commerce, allows AI systems to search for products, compare prices, and make purchases with a single prompt from a person. On October 21, 2026, Walmart announced a new system that would allow AI tools from other companies to shop directly through its website. This decision contrasts with Amazon, which has blocked outside AI tools from shopping on its platform. While both companies are investing in AI, they disagree on how much control these tools should have. As a result, the growth of AI shopping could change how people buy products online and which companies control those purchases.

To understand this technology, it is first important to look at how AI shopping tools work. Traditional AI can suggest products, but customers must still purchase them themselves. However, newer AI tools can complete the entire process. For example, a customer could tell an AI tool to purchase household supplies for under $100. Traditional AI would simply suggest different products, but this new tool would search for the products, compare prices, and most importantly complete the purchase. Walmart's new system would allow customers to set spending limits, review purchases, and stop the AI from making further purchases. Walmart also plans to keep customers' payment information private.

However, not all companies support allowing outside AI tools to make purchases. Amazon has blocked AI shopping tools from other companies, including Meta. One reason for this decision is because Amazon currently controls how customers find products through advertisements, search results, and suggestions. Essentially, companies pay Amazon to advertise and funnel customers to them. If AI tools begin making these decisions, Amazon could lose some of this control. For example, an AI tool might choose the cheapest product instead of one that a retailer paid to advertise. As a result, Walmart could benefit from allowing outside AI tools to shop on its website, while Amazon could benefit from keeping customers within its own shopping system.

There are also problems that could come from allowing AI to make purchases. An AI tool could misunderstand instructions, buy the wrong product, or spend more money than the customer wanted. Companies such as American Express are developing ways to make sure AI purchases follow customer instructions. However, these systems are still new, and it is unclear whether customers will trust AI to make expensive purchases.

AI shopping could make buying products online faster and easier, especially for products people purchase regularly. However, its success will depend on whether AI tools can make purchases correctly and keep customer information safe. Walmart's announcement is an early example of how online shopping could change, as AI begins to take a larger role in deciding what products people buy.

Could Slow Airplane Wi-Fi Be a Thing of the Past?

Often, flying means being stuck with complimentary or paid Wi-Fi that struggles to load even a webpage. On October 26, 2026, European budget airline easyJet announced that it would introduce Starlink internet across its A320 and A321 fleet. EasyJet will roll out installations in the first half of 2027 and expects to complete them by 2028. EasyJet says that its customers will be able to stream, game, and browse during flights using Starlink internet. This announcement shows how faster internet is becoming a selling point, even for affordability-focused airlines.

Starlink, developed by SpaceX, utilizes a network of satellites in low Earth orbit to deliver internet. These satellites orbit much closer to Earth than traditional geostationary satellites, reducing signal travel time and lowering latency. While download speed determines how fast a file may arrive, latency affects how responsive a connection feels. Lower latency is especially useful in activities where small delays can have a dramatic effect, such as online gaming. Starlink’s approach helps make the experience of participating in these activities more practical in places where ordinary internet infrastructure may be unavailable.

For passengers, better connectivity could make a flight more useful and appealing. A student traveling home could access course materials, while a business traveler could work on shared documents. Aside from work, other passengers may simply stream entertainment or play online games. EasyJet’s announcement is particularly interesting because it brings this technology into a budget-friendly airline. It suggests that speedy, reliable internet may gradually become something travelers expect across all ticket prices, though how airlines price and deliver the service may depend heavily on their approach.

However, installing Wi-Fi on planes also creates significant business challenges, and airlines must weigh whether the benefits justify the equipment and operating costs. Reuters reported that easyJet’s CEO Kenton Jarvis had previously described the economics of offering on-board Wi-Fi as challenging. Additionally, rival airline Ryanair raised concerns about Starlink's costs. EasyJet did not disclose the financial terms of its agreement with Starlink.

EasyJet’s implementation of Starlink reflects a broader technological shift: satellite networks are becoming more integrated into everyday consumer experiences. For travelers, this may mean fewer interruptions between internet experiences on the ground and in the air. The next test will come when installations roll out, and consumers can test the Wi-Fi themselves. If the connection is reliable and reasonably priced, checking if a plane offers good Wi-Fi on a plane may become as routine as comparing baggage fees.

The Vanishing Software Moat

Now, I know this might be difficult for most of us to relate to, but have you ever noticed your friends on Android complaining about apps not being on Google Play Store? I know, I know, just move to iOS, but what if it didn’t have to be that way? What if you could just create the android version for yourself and start using it, especially if these apps don’t require you to interact with other people?

A few weeks ago, a similarly pissed-off developer used Claude to build ArtCraft, a free suite of alternatives to Adobe Photoshop, Illustrator, Premiere Pro, Lightroom, After Effects, InDesign, and Acrobat Pro. Now, I haven't used these products myself, but they're certainly not as polished or capable as Adobe's offerings. What's remarkable is how cheaply and quickly they were developed.

Now, while these software products aren't fully replicable just yet, what happens if and when they become replicable? If I can download an application, create an almost identical clone, and use it for free, why would I continue paying for the original?

Consider two companies. AlphaThirteen spends $5 million and two years researching, designing, and developing specialized software. Just as it approaches profitability, BetaCorp purchases its product, uses AI to recreate its functionality in two weeks, and sells an alternative for a fraction of the price. AlphaThirteen bears the costs of innovation, while BetaCorp captures its benefits. Prima facie, this looks like a first-mover disadvantage.

This raises a broader question: if proprietary code ceases to be a competitive moat, what happens to the economics of software?

I foresee two opposing forces: First, innovation becomes harder to monetize. If competitors can reproduce new functionality almost immediately, incumbents lose pricing power, profit margins compress, and the incentive to invest heavily in original software development diminishes.

Second, innovation becomes more accessible. Anyone with sufficiently specific needs can create their own utilities without substantial technical expertise. The costs of developing software fall for innovators and imitators alike, potentially increasing the total amount of innovation.

Of course, not all software companies are equally vulnerable. Recreating Adobe Photoshop's functionality might eventually eliminate the need to purchase it. But replicating Spotify's interface doesn't grant access to its licensed music catalog. The relevant distinction is between replicable functionality and assets that cannot simply be copied.

In the short term, incumbents retain advantages in reliability, distribution, and customer trust. In the longer term, however, AI could fundamentally change the economics of software.

The implications extend beyond falling subscription prices. Software companies have historically enjoyed premium valuations because of recurring revenues, high margins, and expectations of sustained growth. If AI erodes their pricing power and competitive advantages, investors may assign lower multiples to their future earnings. Venture capital could similarly become less willing to finance easily replicable software products, even as lower development costs make launching startups cheaper.

These concerns are already influencing financial markets. In February 2026, Reuters reported that software and services stocks had lost approximately $830 billion in market capitalization over six trading sessions amid fears of AI disruption. Capital may consequently shift toward businesses with harder-to-replicate assets: proprietary data, distribution networks, licensed content, and computational infrastructure. The winners of the AI software revolution need not be the companies producing the most software.

This leaves us with a rather interesting paradox: we may enter an era in which software creates more economic value than ever before, yet individual software companies capture less of it.
