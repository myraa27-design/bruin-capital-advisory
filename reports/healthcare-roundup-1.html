How the Pharmaceutical Industry Changed Over the Last Month: Global Overview

By: Isaias Plascencia, Jacob Ralph, Myra Agrawal

Overview

The Global Pharmaceutical industry is constantly changing and readjusting. More recently, it is being reshaped by a race for clinical speed. While for decades, drug development has been led by the US and Europe, over the past ten years China has quickly become a major source of clinical trials and innovative drugs, drawing in Western Capital and partnerships. Monitoring the changes in the Pharmaceutical and Healthcare Industry over the last month, this article gives a rundown of the most relevant happenings in the pharmaceutical industry and what drives this change, shedding light on how this impacts the future of pharmaceutical leadership.

USA

On September 15, 2026, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) launched the Expedited Investigational New Drug (IND) Pilot program. The program falls under the broader initiative of Operation Trailblazer, first introduced by the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) in June. This operation tries to accelerate early-stage clinical research and counter a growing trend of drugmakers moving their first human trial studies overseas.

Under this new program, the FDA will select 8 to 10 drugmaker-institution pairings to participate in a streamlined regulatory process. The criteria for these institutions is vague, but they need to be “qualified research institutions.” Applications for this new program close on October 30, 2026. Eligible partners include academic research centers, contract research organizations (CROs), and biotech companies of all sizes, as long as the FDA views them as qualified.

The central feature of the program is a rolling review process. Rather than requiring drug sponsors to submit a massive, single Investigational New Drug application, the agency will look at different scientific components as they are completed. Also, regulatory processes that follow in a sequence, like the ethics board evaluations and clinical trial site preparations, will now be conducted at the same time.

FDA leadership, including CDER Director Mike Davis and CBER Director Karim Mikhail, noted that these changes are intended to limit unnecessary delays, resolve potential protocol issues earlier, and lower the risk of clinical holds before human testing begins.

The program is a direct response to regulatory competition from countries such as Australia and China. Time savings make Australia more appealing, as its streamlined reviews and financial incentives allow trials to begin in under 70 days, whereas moving from scientific discovery to first-in-human trials in the U.S. traditionally takes up to two years. This program further targets a shift to China, where regulatory reforms and heavy investment created a situation where annual clinical trial starts in China surged from under 1,000 in 2010 to over 5,000 in 2024, eclipsing the low U.S. baseline of approximately 3,500 trials per year.

Health officials warn that this offshore migration threatens U.S. leadership in biotechnology, risking the loss of intellectual property, venture investment, top scientific talent, and early patient access to breakthrough treatments.

Industry groups such as the Biotechnology Innovation Organization (BIO) have acknowledged that a rolling review framework could offer substantial time savings. However, some stakeholders remain cautious, expressing concerns that pairing sponsors with specific research institutions might introduce extra administrative layers rather than simplifying the path to human trials.

Europe

On Tuesday, September 22nd, nine European top pharmaceutical boards (AstraZeneca, Boehringer Ingelheim, Chiesi, GSK, Ipsen, Novo Nordisk, Novartis, Roche, and Sanofi) convened and pressed for government policy changes to continue sustainability for pharmaceuticals.

This rally comes after Europe's decline in contribution to the global pharmaceutical industry’s research and development. In the 1990s, Europe contributed to about ~43% of the global pharmaceutical industry’s drug development. Today, we see that number fall to 31% and expect to see a continued decline. Additionally, Europe’s clinical study shares have since halved in a decade and currently stands at a measly 9%.

The main causes for this constant decline can be attributed to slow clinical development and budget caps. Simply put, Europe doesn’t have the policy implementation to match par with pharmaceutical giants such as the US and China (both countries contributing ~$600B+ in pharma investment); capped budgets place heavy limitations on research and development spending. Notably, a fundamental issue remains prevalent: European member states see pharmaceutical drug development as a burden and expense rather than an investment. This unwillingness to allocate budget for research and development has been a major contributor to the decline in development, and top boards push for these countries to reevaluate investment in development through incentives.

The incentives mainly target three areas:

  1. Accelerating clinical trials & reasonable digital policy
  2. Protecting intellectual property to incentivize protection of innovative developments
  3. Providing financial incentives and flexibility to a majority of member states

These incentives and revisions have been summarized in a push by these boards called the European Biotech Act. However, the underlying fundamental value of clinical research and development must be understood by member states and their governments to both receive their support and budget allocation. Without this change, it will be incredibly difficult to convince member states to revert their budget back to 1990 levels; focusing on leading the global pharmaceutical industry.

China

Having gone from 5% to 30% in global clinical trials in a decade, China has gone from a minor player to the main reason Europe and the U.S. are rewriting their pharmaceutical policy. Helped by vertical integration and tax incentives, it now runs more WHO-registered clinical trials than the United States.

This growth was planned by the government. Beijing has used subsidies and dedicated hubs, such as Pudong in Shanghai, which aims to become a global center for cell and gene therapy research, clinical testing, and manufacturing. Speed is China’s biggest advantage. From September 2025, the NMPA can clear eligible innovative-drug trial applications in 30 working days, and priority review puts marketing applications on a 130-working-day clock. In June 2026, the NMPA and three other departments issued updated Good Clinical Practice guidelines, replacing the version in place from 2020. In October, the regulator went further and proposed a market exclusivity framework offering up to seven years of protection for rare disease therapies and two years for pediatric drugs. The results are showing: 59 innovative drugs and 51 innovative medical devices have been approved so far in 2026, including 13 first-in-class drugs.

Western capital has followed innovation. Cross-border licensing deals between Chinese biotechs and global pharma reached a record $137 billion in 2025, almost ten times the $13.9 billion recorded in 2021. By mid-September 2026, overseas licensing deals for Chinese innovative drugs had already passed $120 billion. Major buyers include AbbVie, Roche, and Eli Lilly, which licensed assets from RemeGen, MediLink, and Innovent, respectively.

Washington has pushed back. The BIOSECURE Act became law in December 2025, and in February the U.S. International Trade Commission opened an investigation into Chinese state support, subsidies, and pricing in biotech. In June, the proposed Biotech Investment National Security Act (BINSA) would make licensing deals, joint ventures, and equity investments subject to Treasury review. This creates a clear tension. Western pharma relies more and more on Chinese pipelines, while Western governments try to limit that same dependence.

Conclusion

Across all three regions, one variable now determines competitiveness: how quickly a drug can move from discovery to first-in-human trials. Capital, talent, and intellectual property flow to wherever that time is shortest.

Each region is responding from a different position. Europe is losing ground. Its share of global R&D fell from around 43% in the 1990s to 31%, and its clinical study share has halved to 9%. The industry’s proposed answer, the European Biotech Act, relies on incentives and IP protection. However, it depends on member states treating drug development as an investment rather than a budget burden, and that shift has not happened yet. The U.S. is using a two-sided strategy. Offensively, Operation Trailblazer and the Expedited IND Pilot try to win back early-stage trials through rolling review and parallel processes. Defensively, the BIOSECURE Act and the proposed BINSA aim to limit the flow of American capital into Chinese biotech.

China, meanwhile, has set the pace. Its regulatory speed, state backing, and new exclusivity protections have made it a source of innovation for Western pharma, not just a low-cost manufacturer.

The key risk is that these strategies may conflict. U.S. restrictions could protect national security while raising costs for American pharma companies that now depend on Chinese assets for their pipelines. Europe’s reforms, if member states don’t fund them, could leave the region squeezed between two faster competitors. Over the next few months, the signals to watch are:

  • How many sponsors apply before the FDA pilot closes on October 30
  • Whether BINSA or a COINS Act biotech designation moves forward
  • Whether EU governments back the Biotech Act with real budget commitments

Ultimately, leadership in pharmaceuticals will go to the region that treats speed and investment as policy priorities rather than afterthoughts. Right now, China is the one setting that standard.
